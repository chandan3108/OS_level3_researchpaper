How to read this replay

Queue length, capacity and temperature are sampled at roughly 100 ms. Gain updates use completion events at their actual simulated times. A job released before 10 s may finish after 10 s, so the new error pattern does not instantly replace every observation.

The 20–40 s tail includes finishing work and idle energy. No host-CPU sensors are read. Capacity 0.4 is a normalized service rate—not 40% of your Mac’s physical clock.

THE SEVEN RULES

Same history. Different update schedules.

What EDF and capacity selection do

Ready tasks are ordered by the earliest deadline. For each deadline prefix, the controller estimates the work remaining, adds a 10% margin, and rounds up to a supported capacity: 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 or 1.0.

When an estimate is exhausted or a task is late, recovery requests maximum capacity at the next decision. Common thermal protection can reduce that request.

What “Apple-inspired” actually means

One worker executes one task at a time. The 0.4 state is labeled E mode; 0.6/0.8/1.0 are P modes. E-mode active power is assumed to be 0.65 times the generic formula at that capacity.

There is no multicore parallelism, ARM instruction emulation or XNU scheduler implementation. The numeric model is uncalibrated.

THE CLAIM, AND ITS BOUNDARIES

The paper behind the demo.

A reproducible study of when completion-by-completion correction earns its cost relative to batch or rolling-window recalibration.

RESEARCH QUESTION

When does continued correction help beyond practical recalibration?

Every primary strategy starts from the same twenty completed historical observations. We test changing biases, stable errors, noise and mixed families, then retain the cases where feedback helps, trades off or loses.

FOLLOW THE EVIDENCE

From a workload to a reported result.

1. Generate matched inputs

Separate seeded streams create task arrivals/work and estimation errors. Every controller sees the same task, estimate and calibration-history hashes.

2. Execute the whole workload

One preemptive worker, 20 s of arrivals, a shared 40 s accounting window, and complete drain. Late work is retained and idle energy is counted.

3. Save one row per strategy

Energy, misses, modeled overhead, response latency, temperatures and correction counts are saved with seeds and source fingerprints.

4. Compare paired outcomes

Compare feedback with each baseline on the same seed. Average over twenty paired streams. Variable differences get pointwise bootstrap intervals.

The statistical claim is narrow.

The declared family compares feedback against Batch 20 in two shift scenarios and two profiles, with ordinary cooling, bursty arrivals and modeled costs: eight endpoint tests.

One-sided sign tests count negative, positive and tied pairs. Holm correction covers those eight tests. It does not make all other comparisons confirmatory or prove deadline noninferiority.

A missed deadline has context.

Every tested irregular/Poisson stream has at least one miss even under maximum-capacity EDF with no overhead or thermal cap. Every tested bursty stream is zero-miss feasible under that diagnostic.

This does not excuse extra controller misses. The retained-job EDF miss count is not a lower bound on every scheduling policy.

The complete eight-test family

Paper data · 20 paired streams per condition

Signs are feedback minus Batch 20. Counts show lower / higher / tied outcomes. Tests assess direction among non-ties, not mean-effect magnitude. A non-significant timing result does not establish safety or equivalence.

FINAL EXPLORATORY COST CHECK

Batch 5 at the same learning cost.

Post hoc · outside the eight-test family

Both methods pay 50 µs at every completion, with no extra batch-application charge. Ordinary cooling and bursty arrivals; 20 paired workloads per row. Differences are feedback minus Batch 5: positive energy means Batch 5 uses less.

The check contains 80 paired workloads and 160 policy executions, reusing seeds 200–219. It tests an assumed cost treatment; it does not measure implementation costs.
